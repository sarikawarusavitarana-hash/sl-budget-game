Sri Lanka's economy is recovering from the 2022 economic crisis, with real GDP growing 5% in 2025 and 4.7% in the first half of 2026. Inflation, which exceeded 50% during the crisis, has fallen sharply but has risen again to 8% in August and September 2026. Government revenue has improved, but public debt remains high at around 100% of GDP, while debt repayments continue to put pressure on government finances. Many households are still recovering from the crisis, with poverty remaining above pre-crisis levels. Sri Lanka also faces new pressures from energy prices, global uncertainty and climate-related shocks.

As Finance Minister, your challenge is to balance economic growth, social protection, investment and fiscal discipline. The choices you make will shape Sri Lanka's future growth, debt sustainability and resilience.

Your challenge

You have nine weeks to shape Sri Lanka's next Budget and help secure the country's economic recovery.

You are responsible for managing a national budget of around LKR 5 trillion each year and balance spending with revenue, maintain fiscal discipline, and meet critical targets such as a primary surplus and long-term debt sustainability.

You will not undertake this task alone, but every decision you make will influence Sri Lanka's economic stability and future trajectory.

Minister, we're counting on you to build a strong team around this Budget.

We've committed to clear fiscal targets, including improving revenue, maintaining a primary surplus, and managing debt carefully, so keep an eye on the indicators, you don't want things slipping off track. We've set your starting point based on the latest Budget framework, but the choices ahead are yours. And remember, the public will be watching closely.

One more thing to keep in mind: the IMF programme behind many of these targets ends in March 2027, and the Government has not yet decided whether to seek a successor.

Taxes

Before deciding how to spend the money, decide how the Government will raise it. The 2026 Budget focuses on increasing domestic revenue, improving tax collection through the new Revenue Administration Management Information System, and raising tax revenue to more than 15 per cent of gross domestic product over the medium term. The Government has set out a total revenue and grants target of 5,300 billion rupees. How will you raise the money needed to fund your Budget?

While defence spending has remained relatively stable in recent years, there is increasing debate over whether resources should continue at current levels or be redirected towards sectors such as education, health, and economic development.

Week Five: Energy and Subsidies

Energy spending covers electricity, fuel support, renewables and state-owned utilities. Since the crisis, Sri Lanka has moved towards cost-reflective pricing — and in 2026 the Middle East war disrupted oil shipments and sent prices sharply higher. Between March and May, petrol and diesel prices rose by roughly half and electricity tariffs by about a third, and fuel was rationed, helping push inflation to 8%. The IMF is urging cost-recovery pricing, while households and businesses want targeted relief. Your decisions shape spending, living costs, energy security and the Budget's long-term position.

Week Seven: Agriculture

Agriculture remains central to Sri Lanka's economy and food security, sustaining rural livelihoods and underpinning key exports such as tea, rubber and coconut, with rice as the country's staple crop. The sector is facing a run of climate shocks: Cyclone Ditwah's catastrophic flooding and landslides in late 2025 damaged paddy, vegetable crops, livestock and inland fisheries nationwide, and a strengthening El Niño has brought dry weather this year, with a dry spell forecast again for early 2027. Farm output fell 2.3% in the second quarter, and the Government has already brought the Maha planting season forward. Your decisions will determine how much the Government invests in recovery, irrigation and resilience, while balancing fiscal sustainability.

Week Eight: Public Administration

Public administration keeps Government services running, funding public sector salaries, pensions, ministries, and essential administrative functions. In Sri Lanka, these costs make up a large share of recurrent expenditure, leaving limited room for change. The final phase of the 2025 public-sector pay revision takes effect in January 2027, so part of next year's wage bill is already committed. Your decisions will determine whether to improve efficiency and create fiscal space, or maintain current spending to support public service delivery.

Week Nine: Environment and Disaster Management

Protecting Sri Lanka's environment and preparing for natural disasters is becoming increasingly important as climate change increases the frequency of floods, droughts, landslides, and cyclones. Government spending supports disaster preparedness, emergency response, ecosystem protection, and climate resilience. Your decisions will determine whether Sri Lanka invests more in preventing future disasters or responding after they occur, while balancing limited public finances.

After Cyclone Ditwah, Parliament approved a Rs. 500 billion supplementary estimate and the IMF provided US$206 million in emergency financing, but in June the National Audit Office reported that nothing had yet been disbursed through the Rebuilding Sri Lanka initiative. With a strengthening El Niño, above-average rain is also forecast for October and November after the dry months.
